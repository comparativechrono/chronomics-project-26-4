Everything so far rests on one dataset, built one way: short reads aligned to one reference, with the phase worked out statistically. Now test the whole result against genomes assembled from long reads, where the two chromosomes are read separately and nothing is inferred.

You will learn
  • what a pangenome is and why one reference genome is not enough
  • the difference between genotyping against a reference and assembling a genome
  • to replicate a finding in independent data – the most valuable thing you can do to it
  • why repeats are where short-read methods fail, and how to show it

1. Why a second dataset, and why this one

The 1000 Genomes genotypes are inferred in two ways. Reads a few hundred bases long are aligned to the GRCh38 reference, so anything that differs too much from the reference is hard to see at all; and the assignment of alleles to the two chromosomes – the phase – comes from a statistical model, not from observation.

The Human Pangenome Reference Consortium takes a different approach: for each person, long reads are assembled into two complete chromosome sequences, one per parent, and the assemblies are combined into a graph. Nothing is imputed. Where the two datasets agree, the finding is solid; where they disagree, the disagreement is informative.

A pangenome in one paragraph

A single reference genome is one person's sequence, and every variant is described as a difference from it. That works until the thing you care about is absent from the reference, or sits in a region where the reference is unusual. A pangenome graph holds many genomes at once: shared sequence is a single path, and where genomes differ the graph branches. Reads map to the graph rather than to one sequence, so a variant that is common in the species but missing from the reference is no longer invisible.

Use the same definition in both datasets, or you are not comparing them

"Off the haplotype" has to mean the same thing in both scans. It is tempting, in the assemblies, to leave out the sixteen haplotypes that carry rs41310749 alone – they are a different background, after all. But if you do that here and not in stage 5, the two scans are answering slightly different questions, and one variant (rs6807191, at 38,658,460) passes the criterion only because of that choice: off-haplotype 0.0985 under the narrower definition and 0.1002 under the wider one. A membership list that depends on a bookkeeping decision is not a finding. Use all non-carriers in both, as stage 5 did, and say so.

That is the replication

Two datasets, different people, different sequencing technology, different analysis pipeline, no shared assumptions about phase – and the same block, with the same boundaries. The 50.6 kb figure is now something you can defend. This is worth more than any amount of additional analysis of the first dataset.

What a 0.0008 miss tells you

In the assemblies all three variants are on every single carrier haplotype – 40 out of 40. In the short-read panel they are on 90–93% of carriers and on a few per cent too many non-carriers. That pattern is the fingerprint of imperfect phasing: a small number of alleles assigned to the wrong chromosome of the pair blurs the edges of a block in both directions at once.

But look at the margins. rs62242851 fails the panel's threshold by eight ten-thousandths, and rs6807191 fails the assemblies' by two ten-thousandths. A threshold is a cliff, and a variant can fall off it for reasons that have nothing to do with biology. Three of the 18 that do pass are within 0.01 of the bound as well.

So the honest description is not "the block has 18 members" or "20". It is: 18 variants meet the criterion in both datasets; two more meet it in the assemblies only; and the criterion is doing more work than the data can support at the edges. Say which list you used and show the near-misses. In stage 10 you will see that one of these borderline variants carries the project's strongest trait association – which is exactly why this bookkeeping matters.

4. The repeat that short reads could not describe

The third disagreement is of a different kind. Look at what each dataset says is at 38,667,308:

echo "--- 1000 Genomes:"
bcftools view -H -r chr3:38667308 /data/examples/scn5a/1000g/scn5a_locus.vcf.gz | cut -f 1-5
echo "--- HPRC assemblies:"
bcftools view -H -r chr3:38667308 /data/examples/scn5a/hprc/scn5a_locus.vcf.gz | cut -f 1-5
Check

The short-read panel has five records at that position, each proposing a different number of TA units: G>GTA, G>GTATA, G>GTATATA, G>GTGTA and GTA>G. The assemblies have one call, a four-base TA expansion, carried by every haplotype of the block. This is a short tandem repeat, and it is the single worst case for short-read genotyping: a read that is shorter than the repeat cannot tell you how many copies there are, and the caller ends up offering several incompatible descriptions of the same thing.

Think

None of the 18 variants you found in stage 5 would have been missed by short reads. This one was. How many variants of this kind do you think are missing from every short-read study ever published – and does that change how you read a paper that says "no variant in this region was associated"?

5. Is there anything big on the block?

A 50 kb haplotype could carry a deletion, a duplication or an inversion that no short-read study would show. Assemblies can answer that. Look for anything 50 bases or longer:

len <- abs(nchar(raw[[3]]) - nchar(raw[[2]]))
big <- which(len >= 50 & pos2 >= 38631501 & pos2 <= 38682101)
length(big)
data.frame(pos = pos2[big], length = len[big],
           on = round(f_on2[big], 3), off = round(f_off2[big], 3))
Check

Nothing. Across the whole 50.6 kb there is no insertion or deletion of 50 bases or more that is carried by the block. Whatever this haplotype does, it does not do it by gaining or losing a large piece of sequence. That is a real negative result, and only the assemblies could have given it to you.
