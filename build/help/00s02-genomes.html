Stage 2 · Two thousand five hundred genomes

Weeks 2–3about 5 hours

Two variants and 2,504 people. Before asking how the variants travel together, find out how common each one is – and learn that "how common" is a question with five different answers.

You will learn
  • what the 1000 Genomes Project is, and what its populations are
  • PLINK 2: turning a VCF into its own fast format, filtering, counting alleles

1. The people

The 1000 Genomes Project sequenced people from 26 populations, grouped into five superpopulations: AFR (African), AMR (admixed American), EAS (East Asian), EUR (European) and SAS (South Asian). This bundle uses the 2,504 who are unrelated to each other.

head -3 /data/examples/scn5a/1000g/samples.tsv
cut -f 3 /data/examples/scn5a/1000g/samples.tsv | tail -n +2 | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn
cut -f 2 /data/examples/scn5a/1000g/samples.tsv | tail -n +2 | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head -6
Check

Five superpopulations, 26 populations: 661 AFR, 504 EAS, 503 EUR, 489 SAS and 347 AMR – 2,504 in all. The largest single populations have about a hundred people each.

These labels are coarse

"European" covers Finns, Tuscans, Britons, Iberians and Utah residents of northern European ancestry. "African" includes West Africans and African-Americans, whose genomes carry European ancestry too. The labels are useful for a first look and misleading if pushed. When something interesting turns up at the superpopulation level, always look at the 26 populations underneath.

2. PLINK 2

bcftools reads VCF files; PLINK is built to do arithmetic across thousands of people and variants at once. Its first job is to convert the VCF into its own three files, which are much faster to read:

cd ~/project
plink2 --vcf /data/examples/scn5a/1000g/scn5a_locus.vcf.gz --make-pgen --out scn5a
ls -la scn5a.*
head -3 scn5a.pvar
head -3 scn5a.psam

scn5a.pgen holds the genotypes packed tightly, scn5a.pvar the variants, scn5a.psam the people, and scn5a.log a record of exactly what was done – keep your logs, they are the honest account of your analysis.

Check

2,504 samples, and about 2,392 variants – the ones carried by at least 1% of chromosomes in this 500 kb; rarer ones were left out to keep the file small. Read scn5a.log rather than trusting the number: one record in this file has the symbolic allele <DEL> instead of a sequence, and tools disagree about whether to count it. bcftools view -H … | wc -l gives 2,392; something that insists on real sequence will give 2,391.

Read the log, every time

Each PLINK run writes a .log file saying how many variants and samples it actually used and what it skipped. Two tools given the same file can honestly disagree, and the log is where you find out. A number you took from a tool without reading its log is a number you cannot defend.

3. How common are they?

--freq counts alleles. --snp picks a single variant by its identifier – the identifiers here are built from position and alleles, like 3:38651526:G:A – and --chr with --from-bp/--to-bp takes a whole stretch. Try both:

plink2 --pfile scn5a --snp 3:38651526:G:A --freq --out one_variant
cat one_variant.afreq

Now the whole promoter stretch, so you can see what else is nearby:

plink2 --pfile scn5a --chr 3 --from-bp 38650537 --to-bp 38651526 --freq --out promoter
cat promoter.afreq
Check

Eight variants lie in that 989-base stretch. The two you care about are at the ends: rs41310239 at 38,650,537 with an alternative allele frequency of 0.1116, and rs41310749 at 38,651,526 with 0.1390.

Note that position 38,650,537 carries two entries: an insertion of two bases, and the A-to-G change that is rs41310239. A position in a VCF file is not the same thing as a variant, and a careless region query will pick up both. Check the REF and ALT columns, always.

Think

The 2012 paper said the two variants were in complete linkage disequilibrium – never apart. If that were exactly true everywhere, what would have to be true of these two frequencies?

4. The same question, five answers

--loop-cats repeats an analysis within each category of a column. Add the population labels to the sample file first. The psam file wants a column called SUPERPOP; build it with awk and join:

awk -F'\t' 'BEGIN{OFS="\t"; print "#IID","SUPERPOP"} NR>1 {print $1,$3}' \
  /data/examples/scn5a/1000g/samples.tsv > superpop.txt
head -3 superpop.txt
plink2 --pfile scn5a --chr 3 --from-bp 38650537 --to-bp 38651526 \
       --pheno superpop.txt --loop-cats SUPERPOP --freq --out bypop
ls bypop.*.afreq
grep -H 38651526 bypop.*.afreq | cut -f 1,5,6
Check

For rs41310749: AFR 0.130, AMR 0.144, EAS 0.068, EUR 0.163, SAS 0.196. For rs41310239: AFR 0.015, AMR 0.134, EAS 0.088, EUR 0.163, SAS 0.197.

The first real result

Look at the African column. rs41310749 is at 13% there – as common as anywhere – but rs41310239 is at 1.5%. Everywhere else the two frequencies are nearly equal. Two variants that are supposedly inseparable are plainly not inseparable in Africa.

That single observation shapes the rest of the project. Keep it in front of you.

5. Doing it yourself

PLINK is quick, but you should know what it is counting. An allele frequency is just the number of alternative alleles divided by the number of chromosomes. Count them straight from the genotypes:

bcftools query -f '[%GT ]\n' -r chr3:38651526 /data/examples/scn5a/1000g/scn5a_locus.vcf.gz \
  | tr ' ' '\n' | grep -c '1'
bcftools query -f '[%GT ]\n' -r chr3:38651526 /data/examples/scn5a/1000g/scn5a_locus.vcf.gz \
  | tr '|' '\n' | tr ' ' '\n' | grep -c '^1$'
Check

The second command counts single alleles and gives 696. There are 2 × 2,504 = 5,008 chromosomes, so the frequency is 696 / 5008 = 0.139 – what PLINK said. The first command gives 635: that is the number of people carrying at least one copy, because it counts genotype strings rather than alleles. The two questions have different answers (635 people, 696 alleles, because 61 people carry two), and mixing them up is a classic way to produce a frequency that is quietly wrong.

Your turn

Work out the frequency of rs41310239 the same way by hand, and then its frequency in Africans only. For the second you will need to keep only African samples: build a list with awk and pass it to bcftools view -S.

Show a hint 
awk -F'\t' 'NR>1 && $3=="AFR" {print $1}' /data/examples/scn5a/1000g/samples.tsv > afr.txt
wc -l afr.txt
bcftools view -S afr.txt -r chr3:38650537 /data/examples/scn5a/1000g/scn5a_locus.vcf.gz \
  | bcftools query -f '[%GT ]\n' | tr '|' '\n' | tr ' ' '\n' | grep -c '^1$'

661 Africans, so 1,322 chromosomes. Careful: that region query returns both records at the position, so keep only the one whose REF is A and ALT is G – awk -F'\t' '$4=="A" && $5=="G"'. It has 20 alternative alleles, giving 20/1322 = 0.0151.
