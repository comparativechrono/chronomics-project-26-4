What those two rows mean together

In stage 3 you worked out from D′ = 1 that rs41310749-A is the older allele and rs41310239-G arose later on chromosomes that already carried it. This table shows you the same event from the other side: Africa still carries the ancestral background at 11.5%, almost none of it carrying the younger variant. Everywhere else, essentially every copy of the background comes with the whole block attached.

The simplest reading: the block was assembled in a population ancestral to non-Africans, or in Africa shortly before the migrations out of it, and the ancestral background it was built on is still present in Africa today.

In the West African populations the haplotype is at 0–1.5% while the lone allele is at 9–15%. In ACB and ASW the haplotype is at 3.1% and 4.9% – higher, because those populations have recent non-African ancestry. That gradient is itself evidence: the block arrives in Africa with admixture, not from African ancestry.

Be careful what you conclude from a frequency difference

A variant commoner in one continent than another is the normal state of affairs. Of the thousands of variants in your file, most will show differences of this size, because human populations have different histories of drift, bottlenecks and migration. A frequency difference is a fact to explain, not evidence of selection, and certainly not evidence of anything about the people who carry it. The explanation here – one allele older than the other, a block assembled on it, and migration – requires no selection at all.

2. How long is it, really? Extended haplotype homozygosity

r² and the on/off scan both needed a threshold. Extended haplotype homozygosity (EHH) does not. Take the carrier chromosomes, walk outwards from the core, and at each distance ask: if I pick two carriers at random, what is the chance they are still identical all the way out to here? It starts at 1 and decays as recombination breaks the block up.

ehh <- function(H, idx, order_out) {
  sapply(seq_along(order_out), function(k) {
    sub <- H[order_out[1:k], idx, drop = FALSE]
    key <- apply(sub, 2, paste, collapse = "")
    n <- table(key)
    sum(n * (n - 1)) / (length(key) * (length(key) - 1))   # homozygosity of extended haplotypes
  })
}

Two curves that could hardly be more different. The non-carriers fall below EHH = 0.5 within about 0.4 kb. The carriers stay above 0.5 for about 33 kb going towards SCN10A and about 21 kb going towards SCN5A. The block is tens of times more homogeneous than its neighbourhood.

Reading length as age

Recombination breaks haplotypes up at a roughly constant rate, so a long, unbroken haplotype is a young one – there has not been time to break it. As a rough guide, a haplotype of length L in morgans has an expected age of about 1/L generations. At roughly 1 centimorgan per megabase here, 50 kb is about 0.05 cM = 0.0005 M, giving an order-of-magnitude age of a couple of thousand generations – tens of thousands of years.

Treat that as an order of magnitude and nothing more. The calculation assumes a constant recombination rate, no selection, a single origin and a simple demography, and the real confidence interval is very wide. But it is the right order: old enough to be spread across Eurasia, young enough not to have been broken up.

Think

A long haplotype at high frequency is the classic signature of positive selection – it is what tests like iHS and XP-EHH look for. You have a long haplotype at 16% in Europeans. What else would you need to show before claiming selection? What is the most boring explanation that also fits?

3. The boring explanation, tested

The boring explanation is drift: with no selection at all, some haplotypes drift up in frequency, and a young one that drifts up is long. To argue for selection you must show the haplotype is longer than expected for its frequency, which means comparing it with other haplotypes of the same frequency elsewhere in the genome – a genome-wide scan, which your 500 kb cannot support.

What you can and cannot say

You can say: this haplotype is unusually homogeneous compared with other chromosomes at this locus. You cannot say it is under selection, because you have not compared it with the rest of the genome at matched frequency. Say the first and not the second – and say explicitly in your report that you did not test for selection. A sentence admitting what you did not do is worth more than a paragraph implying you did.
