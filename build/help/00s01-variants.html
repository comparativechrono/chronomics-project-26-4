Stage 1 · Two variants

Weeks 1–2about 4 hours

Start where the project starts: two lines in a database. Find them in the data you have been given, find out what the world already knows about them, and learn enough of the terminal to go on.

You will learn
  • the terminal: moving around, looking into files, joining commands with pipes
  • what a VCF file is, and how to pull one position out of one
  • that a position means nothing without saying which build of the genome it is on
  • to look a variant up in Ensembl and dbSNP, and a paper up in Europe PMC

1. Your first commands

The Terminal tab on the right is a Linux terminal running in this browser. A command is a program's name, then its arguments. Try these one at a time:

pwd
ls /data/examples/scn5a
mkdir -p ~/project/results
cd ~/project
pwd

pwd prints where you are; ls lists a folder; mkdir -p makes one (and does not complain if it is already there); cd moves you. ~ is your home folder.

2. The table of variants

The project's variants are in a small table. column -t lines the columns up so you can read it:

head -3 /data/examples/scn5a/variants.tsv
column -t -s $'\t' /data/examples/scn5a/variants.tsv | head -5
wc -l /data/examples/scn5a/variants.tsv
Check

The first two rows are the two variants of the 2012 paper, rs41310749 and rs41310239. The rest – the file has 24 lines in all, so 23 variants – are the ones you are going to discover for yourself over the next five stages. Do not look at them too closely yet.

Notice that each variant has two positions, pos_GRCh38 and pos_GRCh37. A position is only meaningful together with the version – the build – of the human reference genome it refers to. GRCh38 came out in 2013; a great deal of published data, including most of the GWAS files you will use in stage 9, is still on the older GRCh37.

cut -f 1,3,4 /data/examples/scn5a/variants.tsv | head -3
awk -F'\t' 'NR>1 {print $4 - $3}' /data/examples/scn5a/variants.tsv | sort -u
Check

Every variant has exactly the same difference between the two builds: 41,491. That is not a general rule – it holds along this stretch of chromosome 3 and nowhere else. Write it in your logbook; you will need it in stage 9, and a project of this kind has sunk on a build mix-up before now.

The most common mistake in this field

If a result looks strange, check the build before you check anything else. A 41 kb shift will not make a variant vanish – it will quietly give you a different variant, with plausible-looking numbers.

3. Finding them in the genotypes

The genotypes of 2,504 people live in a VCF file. It is a text file: a header of lines beginning ##, then one line per variant, then one column per person. bcftools reads it. -H drops the header; -r takes a region:

ls -la /data/examples/scn5a/1000g/
bcftools view -H -r chr3:38650537 /data/examples/scn5a/1000g/scn5a_locus.vcf.gz | cut -f 1-5
bcftools view -H -r chr3:38651526 /data/examples/scn5a/1000g/scn5a_locus.vcf.gz | cut -f 1-5

Two lines, each giving the chromosome, position, an identifier, the reference allele (the letter in the reference genome) and the alternative allele.

Check

At 38,650,537 the reference is A and the alternative G: that is rs41310239. At 38,651,526 it is G to A: rs41310749. They are 989 bases apart.

Now look at the people. Each genotype is two numbers separated by |: 0 for the reference allele, 1 for the alternative, and the bar means the data are phased – it is known which of the person's two chromosomes carries which.

bcftools view -H -r chr3:38651526 /data/examples/scn5a/1000g/scn5a_locus.vcf.gz | cut -f 10-14
bcftools query -f '[%GT ]\n' -r chr3:38651526 /data/examples/scn5a/1000g/scn5a_locus.vcf.gz | tr ' ' '\n' | sort | uniq -c
Check

Four genotype strings: 1,869 people are 0|0, 284 are 0|1, 290 are 1|0 and 61 are 1|1 – 2,504 people in all. Most carry none of the alternative allele, about one in five carries one, and 61 carry two.

Think

Why are 0|1 and 1|0 listed separately, when both mean "one copy"? What extra thing does the phasing let you ask, that an unphased genotype would not? (This is the whole basis of the project.)

4. What the world already knows

Open the Databases tab and look rs41310749 up in Ensembl, then in dbSNP. Write down: its alleles, its frequency in each population, and what consequence Ensembl assigns it.

Then find the paper. In the Europe PMC card, search for the variant identifier itself – a good trick, because a paper that reports a variant usually names it.

Check

Only a handful of papers mention these variants at all. The one you want is Park et al., Heart Rhythm 2012;9:1090–1096 – one kindred carrying an SCN5A loss-of-function mutation, 22 mutation carriers, in whom the two promoter variants were associated with more severe arrhythmia (P = 0.0007). Read it: how many carriers, how many severe cases, what they measured, and how strong you think the evidence is.

The sentence this project is built on

In their methods: a "systematic survey of the 2.8-kb SCN5A promoter region". That was the window they searched, and it is where they found the two variants.

By stage 5 you will have shown that the two variants belong to a block 50.6 kb long. Which means 95% of it lay outside the window the 2012 study could see – not through any fault of theirs, but because nobody had looked. That gap is the project.

Your turn

Ensembl gives rs41310749 the consequence "regulatory region variant" and rs41310239 "intergenic variant". Both sit between SCN5A and SCN10A. Using the gene table, work out how far each is from the start of SCN5A – and remember that SCN5A is on the minus strand, so its start is its highest coordinate.

Show the answer 
cat /data/examples/scn5a/annotation/genes.tsv
awk -F'\t' 'NR>1 && $1=="SCN5A" {print "SCN5A starts (minus strand) at", $4}' /data/examples/scn5a/annotation/genes.tsv

SCN5A runs from 38,548,057 to 38,649,743 on the minus strand, so transcription starts at 38,649,743 and runs leftwards. rs41310239 is 794 bases upstream of it, rs41310749 is 1,783 bases upstream. Both are in the promoter region the 2012 authors sequenced.

For your logbook
  • The commands you learned, each with one line on what it does – your own cheat sheet.
  • The two variants: position on both builds, alleles, distance apart, distance from the gene.
  • The build offset, 41,491, and why it matters.
  • A paragraph on the 2012 paper: what it claims, and what would have to be true for the claim to hold.

Going further

  • The third allele. Ensembl lists rs41310239 as A/G/T. The 1000 Genomes file has only A and G. Which sources report a T, and at what frequency? What would you need to see before believing in a third allele?
  • How many variants are there in total? Count the lines in the VCF with bcftools view -H … | wc -l (you should get 2,392) and work out how many variants per kilobase that is across the 500 kb. Remember that this file keeps only variants carried by at least 1% of chromosomes – the real number is far higher.
  • Two records, one position. bcftools view -H … | cut -f2 | sort | uniq -d | wc -l counts the positions that appear more than once: there are 75. Look at a few. What is different about the two records at the same position, and which column tells you? You have already met one such pair – position 38,650,537 – and you will meet it again in stage 2, where ignoring it gives the wrong answer.
  • The odd one out. One record in this file has <DEL> in its ALT column instead of a sequence of letters. Find it with bcftools view -H … | awk -F'\t' '$5 ~ /</' | cut -f 1-5. That is a symbolic allele: the file is saying "a deletion starts here" without spelling out what is deleted. Some tools count it as a variant and some quietly drop it, which is why two honest programs can give you 2,392 and 2,391 for the same file.
