Two letters, and the fifty thousand that travel with them

Week 1about 3 hours

In 2012 a study of one family found two changes in the switch that turns on the heart's sodium channel gene, SCN5A. The two changes were always inherited together, and family members who carried them had worse arrhythmia. Since then they have been treated as a pair. But a pair of letters in the genome is rarely alone: it is usually a fragment of a much longer stretch that is passed on intact, generation after generation. This project asks what that longer stretch is, what else rides on it, and whether it does anything.

The question

Two variants, rs41310749 and rs41310239, sit about a thousand letters upstream of SCN5A. They were reported to be in complete linkage disequilibrium – never seen apart. Linkage disequilibrium does not stop at two variants, though: it falls away gradually with distance, so a pair in perfect step is almost always the middle of a haplotype block, a run of DNA that recombination has not yet broken up.

Your project: find the block. Use the genomes of 2,504 people to measure how far the two variants' company extends, work out exactly which other variants travel with them, check the answer in long-read assemblies that read the stretch end to end, and then ask whether the block is associated with how the heart conducts its electrical signal – and whether it has anything to do with the clock.

Nobody has described this haplotype before, as far as we can tell. The guide takes you through the work in eleven stages, about one a week. Each stage teaches the tools it needs; you do not need to have programmed before.

The plan

Stages 1–6 are the core: by the end of stage 6 you will have defined the haplotype and know where it is found. Stages 7–10 each add a separate line of evidence, and stage 10 is the one where you learn to stop. Each stage ends with going further: ideas to follow if you have time.

The science

Week 1about 2 hours reading

Three things to understand before you start: what the gene does, what a haplotype is, and why a chronobiology group is interested in a heart gene.

1. The heart's sodium channel

Every heartbeat begins as an electrical wave. In each working heart muscle cell the wave starts when sodium channels open and let sodium ions rush in, flipping the voltage across the membrane in about a millisecond. That flip opens the channels in the neighbouring cell, and so the signal spreads. How many channels a cell has, and how readily they open, sets how fast the wave travels.

The main cardiac sodium channel is Nav1.5, made by the gene SCN5A on chromosome 3. Less Nav1.5 means a slower wave. On an electrocardiogram that shows up as a longer PR interval (the time from the atria starting to the ventricles starting) and a wider QRS complex (the time the ventricles take to activate).

When Nav1.5 falls far enough, the heart becomes vulnerable. Loss-of-function changes in SCN5A cause Brugada syndrome, in which the electrocardiogram shows a characteristic pattern and the person is at risk of a dangerous ventricular rhythm. The same gene, changed a different way, causes long QT syndrome type 3, and progressive conduction disease. One gene, several diseases, depending on which way the channel is pushed.

Why a promoter matters

Most known SCN5A disease variants change the protein. The two variants of this project do not: they sit upstream of the gene, in the stretch of DNA that controls how much of it is made. A variant there cannot change the shape of the channel, only the number of them. That is a quieter kind of effect – and a plausible one, because how much Nav1.5 a cell has is exactly what sets conduction speed.

2. The 2012 finding

A family carried a loss-of-function mutation in SCN5A. Some members were barely affected; others had serious arrhythmia. Looking for something that might explain the difference, the authors sequenced 2.8 kb of the SCN5A promoter and found two variants, rs41310749 and rs41310239, in complete linkage disequilibrium with each other. The three most severely affected members carried them on both chromosomes. The suggestion was that a promoter that makes less channel, on top of a broken copy of the gene, tips a mild problem into a serious one.

It is one family, and twenty-two mutation carriers. That is thin evidence, as the authors said. It is also the kind of observation that is worth taking seriously enough to test properly, which is what you are going to do.

3. Haplotypes, recombination and linkage disequilibrium

You have two copies of each chromosome, one from each parent. When you make eggs or sperm, the two copies recombine: they swap segments. A segment that is not cut survives intact into the next generation. Over many generations the surviving segments get shorter and shorter, because every generation is another chance to cut.

A stretch of DNA inherited as one intact unit is a haplotype. The variants on it are inherited together, so knowing one tells you the others – this is linkage disequilibrium, LD. Two measures are used, and they answer different questions:

  • D′ asks: has recombination happened between these two variants? It is 1 when some combination of alleles has never been seen, which is what you expect if they have never been separated. D′ can be 1 even when the two variants have very different frequencies.
  • r² asks: if I know one, do I know the other? It is 1 only when the two are perfect proxies – same frequency, always together. r² is the one that matters when you want to use one variant to stand in for another.

You will meet a textbook example in stage 3: the two promoter variants have D′ = 1 in Africans but r² of about 0.1, because one of them is much commoner than the other there. The same pair in Europeans has r² = 1. The pair did not change; the populations did.

Think

If a derived allele arises once, on one chromosome, every copy of it alive today descends from that chromosome. What does that imply about the DNA on either side of it? And what would make that stretch shorter – time, or recombination rate, or both?

4. The awkward neighbour

About 45 kb downstream of SCN5A sits SCN10A, which makes a different sodium channel, Nav1.8. Inside SCN10A there is a stretch that acts as an enhancer for SCN5A: it reaches back and helps switch the neighbouring gene on. A common variant in it, rs6801957, is one of the strongest signals in all of cardiac genetics – it shifts the PR interval by about 3.5 ms per allele, and its association with the PR interval has a p-value around 10−578.

That is a problem for you. When a signal that strong sits 100 kb away, any variant that is even slightly correlated with it will look associated too. Much of stage 10 is about telling a real, separate signal from the shadow of a huge one – and about recognising when the data you can get cannot settle the question.

5. Why a chronobiology group

Two reasons. The first is that the heart has a clock. Cardiac ion channel genes, Scn5a among them, are expressed rhythmically over the day in mice, and the electrocardiogram changes with time of day in people. The second is clinical: in Brugada syndrome, dangerous rhythms happen overwhelmingly at night, often during sleep. A variant that lowers sodium channel expression might plausibly interact with whatever makes the night dangerous.

That is a hypothesis, not a finding, and this project will not prove it. What it can do is ask the simpler question first: in the hundreds of thousands of people with public genetic and sleep data, does this haplotype move any circadian or sleep measure at all? A clean negative is worth having, and it is the honest starting point for anything more ambitious.

The one number to remember

All positions in this bundle are on GRCh38. Four of the eleven GWAS files are on GRCh37, and across this locus the two differ by exactly 41,491 bases: GRCh37 = GRCh38 + 41,491. That offset holds here and nowhere else in the genome. Every build conversion you do in this project is that one subtraction – and getting it wrong gives you a different variant with perfectly plausible numbers.

Two caveats about the assemblies

231 people is a tenth of the 1000 Genomes panel, so every frequency from it has wide confidence intervals – with 40 carriers, a frequency of 0.087 has a 95% interval of roughly 0.063 to 0.117. And the populations are not sampled in the same proportions as the 1000 Genomes panel, so a frequency difference between the two datasets may be about who was sampled rather than about the data.

What is not here, and why

  • Individual-level phenotypes. Nobody's trait values are public. That is why the project uses summary statistics, and it is the reason a student can work with half a million people's data at all.
  • Rare variants. Filtered out of the 1000 Genomes file at 1%. A rare causal variant on this haplotype would be invisible to you.
  • Cardiac expression data with real power. GTEx has a few hundred heart donors and finds no eQTLs for SCN5A there at all. The mechanism question cannot be settled with public data as it stands.
  • Any data you would need ethical approval for. Everything here is openly published. If a question you want to ask needs something else, that is a finding too – write it in your discussion as the next step.

The reading list

Twenty papers, grouped by the stage that needs them. Read the first one properly in week 1; read the rest when the stage comes. For each, the note says what to take from it – you are not expected to understand every paper in full.

How to read a paper you only partly understand

Read the abstract, then the figures with their captions, then the last paragraph of the discussion. That is often enough to know what the paper claims and how. Go to the methods only when you need to do something the paper did. Nobody reads a genetics paper start to finish on the first pass, and pretending otherwise is how people end up not reading them at all.

The paper this project starts from

Read this one closely

Park JK, Martin LJ, Zhang X, Jegga AG, Benson DW. Genetic variants in SCN5A promoter are associated with arrhythmia phenotype severity in patients with heterozygous loss-of-function mutation. Heart Rhythm 2012;9:1090–1096. doi:10.1016/j.hrthm.2012.02.023

One large kindred carrying the SCN5A loss-of-function mutation 1936delC (Q646RfsX5), with 22 mutation carriers. The authors surveyed 2.8 kb of the SCN5A promoter and found rs41310749 and rs41310239, which they describe as being in complete linkage disequilibrium. Carriers of the two-variant allele had more severe arrhythmia phenotypes (P = 0.0007); all three severely affected patients carried it on both the mutant and the wild-type chromosome.

Two things to notice, because the whole project follows from them. First, the window they searched was 2.8 kb – so if the two variants belong to something larger, 95% of it was outside what they could see. Second, "complete linkage disequilibrium" was observed in one kindred; by stage 3 you will know that this statement is true in some populations and false in others.

Think while you read it

Three severe cases out of 22 carriers, P = 0.0007. Work out what test gives that P-value from those numbers, and what the confidence interval on the effect would be. Then ask what you would want to see before changing how a patient is managed. This is not a criticism of the paper – it is a 2012 family study and it reports what it found. It is practice at reading an effect size next to its uncertainty.

Glossary

Every term the project uses. The stage number says where it first matters – a term you have not met yet will make more sense when you get there.

Variants and genomes

allele 1
One of the alternative forms of a position in the genome. At a position where some people have G and some have A, those are the two alleles.
reference and alternative allele 1
The reference allele is the letter at that position in the reference genome; the alternative is the other one. Which is which is a fact about the reference, not about which is commoner or ancestral.
variant 1
A position where the sequence differs between people, together with the alleles. A SNP or single-nucleotide variant changes one letter; an indel inserts or deletes letters; a structural variant is usually defined as 50 bases or more.
rsID 1
A stable identifier for a variant, assigned by dbSNP, like rs41310749. Preferable to a position because it does not change between genome builds.
genome build 1
A version of the reference genome. GRCh37 and GRCh38 number the same positions differently; at this locus the difference is 41,491 bases. A position without a build is meaningless.
VCF 1
Variant Call Format: a text file with a header of ## lines, then one line per variant and one column per person. A position may appear on more than one line if more than one variant is called there.
symbolic allele 2
An allele written as a placeholder such as <DEL> rather than as a sequence. Some tools count these as variants and some drop them, which is why two programs can give different variant counts for the same file.
minor allele frequency (MAF) 2
The frequency of the rarer of the two alleles. The 1000 Genomes file in this bundle keeps only variants with MAF ≥ 0.01.
superpopulation 2
The five continental groupings of the 1000 Genomes populations: AFR, AMR, EAS, EUR, SAS. Coarse labels that hide real structure – always check the 26 populations underneath.

Haplotypes and linkage

haplotype 1
The particular combination of alleles carried on one physical chromosome. A person has two haplotypes at any locus, one from each parent.
phase 1
Knowing which of a person's two chromosomes carries which allele. In a VCF, 0|1 is phased and 0/1 is not. The 1000 Genomes phase is statistically inferred; assembly-based phase is observed.
linkage disequilibrium (LD) 3
The tendency of alleles at nearby positions to be inherited together more often than chance would give. Measured by D, D′ and r².
D 3
pAB − pApB: how much more often two alleles occur together than if they were independent. Hard to interpret alone because its range depends on the frequencies.
D′ 3
D divided by the largest value it could have taken given the frequencies. D′ = 1 means recombination has never been observed to separate the two – which is the signature of one allele having arisen on a chromosome already carrying the other.
r² 3
The squared correlation between the two alleles across chromosomes. r² = 1 means each perfectly predicts the other. r² depends on both recombination and the frequencies, so D′ = 1 with r² = 0.1 is entirely possible – and happens in this project.
haplotype block 4
A stretch of chromosome within which variants are in strong LD and which is usually inherited as a unit. There is no exact definition: a block's boundaries depend on the measure and the threshold you choose, so always state yours.
recombination hotspot 4
A short stretch where crossing-over is far more likely than average. Block boundaries tend to coincide with them.
extended haplotype homozygosity (EHH) 6
Starting from a core, the probability that two randomly chosen carrier chromosomes are still identical out to a given distance. Starts at 1 and decays. A long, slow decay means a young haplotype, because recombination has not had time to break it.
ancestral and derived 3
The ancestral allele is the older one, usually inferred by comparison with other primates; the derived allele arose later by mutation. Not the same as reference and alternative.

The pangenome

short-read sequencing 7
Reading DNA in fragments of a few hundred bases and aligning them to a reference. Cheap and accurate per base, but poor at repeats and at anything very different from the reference.
long-read sequencing and assembly 7
Reading fragments tens of thousands of bases long, enough to reconstruct each chromosome as a continuous sequence without using a reference. The phase comes out of the assembly rather than from a model.
pangenome 7
A reference that holds many genomes at once. Shared sequence is one path and differences are branches, so a variant common in the species but absent from any single reference is still visible.
reference bias 7
The systematic tendency to find variation that resembles the reference and miss variation that does not. The reason a pangenome exists.
short tandem repeat 7
A short motif repeated many times, where the number of copies varies between people. The hardest class of variant for short reads: at 38,667,308 the short-read panel offers five incompatible descriptions of the same repeat and the assemblies give one.
archaic introgression 8
DNA inherited by modern humans from Neanderthals or Denisovans. A long haplotype that is common outside Africa and rare within it fits the profile – which is why it is worth testing, and why this project tests it and finds the answer is no.

Phenotypes and association

GWAS 9
Genome-wide association study: testing every common variant against a trait in a large sample.
summary statistics 9
The per-variant results of a GWAS – effect size, standard error, P-value – published without individual-level data. What makes this project possible.
effect allele 9
The allele that the reported effect size refers to. If a file's effect allele is the other one, the sign of the effect flips. The commonest source of silent errors in this kind of work.
beta and standard error 9
The estimated change in the trait per copy of the effect allele, and its uncertainty. For a binary trait the effect is usually a log-odds.
z score 9
beta divided by its standard error: the signal in units of its own noise. A z of 5 is about P = 6 × 10−7. Easier to reason about than a P-value when comparing variants.
PR interval, QRS duration, QT interval 9
Measurements from the electrocardiogram. The PR interval covers conduction from the atria to the ventricles; QRS duration is the time the ventricles take to depolarise; the QT interval covers depolarisation and recovery. Sodium-channel function affects all three.
Brugada syndrome 9
An inherited arrhythmia syndrome with a characteristic electrocardiogram and a risk of sudden death, most often associated with loss-of-function variants in SCN5A.
multiple testing 9
Doing many tests makes small P-values likely by chance. The correction should be for the number of independent tests – and 21 variants of one haplotype are close to one test, not 21.

Expression and causality

eQTL 8
Expression quantitative trait locus: a variant whose genotype predicts how much of a gene is made. Measured per tissue, so an eQTL in blood says nothing directly about heart.
normalised effect size (NES) 8
GTEx's effect size for an eQTL: the change in normalised expression per copy of the alternative allele. Negative means the allele goes with less expression.
enhancer and promoter 8
A promoter is the sequence immediately before a gene where transcription starts; an enhancer can be far away and acts by contacting the promoter. rs6801957 is an enhancer inside SCN10A that regulates SCN5A – 115 kb away.
lead variant 10
The variant with the strongest association at a locus. Not necessarily the causal one: it is simply the one that happened to be best correlated with whatever is causal.
tagging 10
Showing an association only because of correlation with the real cause. A variant correlated at r with a lead of z = 50 inherits a z of 50r all by itself – which is how a weak correlation with a huge signal manufactures a convincing-looking result.
conditional analysis 10
Estimating what a variant's association would be with the lead variant held fixed, using summary statistics and an LD matrix. Assumes the LD matrix is known exactly, which it is not – and when the lead's z is very large, that assumption fails badly.
LD reference panel 10
The sample used to estimate correlations between variants, standing in for the (unavailable) GWAS sample. Here it is 503 Europeans, so each correlation carries a standard error of about 0.03 – the limitation at the centre of stage 10.
colocalisation 10
Asking whether two traits – or a trait and a gene's expression – share a causal variant at a locus, in a framework that handles the uncertainty properly. The method you would reach for next.

3. D, D′ and r², by hand

Write pA for the frequency of the rs41310749 alternative allele, pB for rs41310239, and pAB for the frequency of chromosomes carrying both. The basic quantity is

D = pAB − pA pB

– how much more often the two travel together than chance would give. D is awkward on its own because its possible range depends on the frequencies, so it is scaled two ways. D′ divides by the largest value D could have taken; r² is the squared correlation between the two alleles.

pA <- mean(a); pB <- mean(b); pAB <- mean(a == 1 & b == 1)
D <- pAB - pA * pB
Dmax <- if (D >= 0) min(pA * (1 - pB), pB * (1 - pA)) else min(pA * pB, (1 - pA) * (1 - pB))
Dprime <- abs(D) / Dmax
r2 <- D^2 / (pA * (1 - pA) * pB * (1 - pB))
c(pA = pA, pB = pB, pAB = pAB, D = D, Dprime = Dprime, r2 = r2)
cor(a, b)^2          # r2 is just the squared correlation
Check

ld <- function(a, b) {
  pA <- mean(a); pB <- mean(b); pAB <- mean(a == 1 & b == 1)
  D <- pAB - pA * pB
  Dmax <- if (D >= 0) min(pA * (1 - pB), pB * (1 - pA)) else min(pA * pB, (1 - pA) * (1 - pB))
  c(n = length(a), pA = round(pA, 4), pB = round(pB, 4),
    Dprime = round(abs(D) / Dmax, 3), r2 = round(D^2 / (pA * (1 - pA) * pB * (1 - pB)), 3))
}

The explanation

D′ = 1 with very different frequencies has one simple reading: the rarer allele arose on a chromosome that already carried the commoner one. Every copy of rs41310239-G sits on an rs41310749-A background, but not the other way round. The older allele is rs41310749-A; the younger one is rs41310239-G, and it arose later, on a subset of those chromosomes.

The 2012 study was of a single kindred – 22 mutation carriers – and within one family two variants this close together will almost always travel together. "Complete linkage disequilibrium" was a fair description of what they could see. It is not a property of the variants: it is a property of the sample they saw them in.

Why pooling destroyed it

r² is a correlation, and a correlation computed across structured groups measures two things at once: whether the alleles travel together within a group, and whether the groups differ in frequency. Africans carry rs41310749 at 13% and almost none of the rest of the block; Europeans carry the whole block at 16%. 2. How long is it, really? Extended haplotype homozygosity

r² and the on/off scan both needed a threshold. Extended haplotype homozygosity (EHH) does not. Take the carrier chromosomes, walk outwards from the core, and at each distance ask: if I pick two carriers at random, what is the chance they are still identical all the way out to here? It starts at 1 and decays as recombination breaks the block up.

ehh <- function(H, idx, order_out) {
  sapply(seq_along(order_out), function(k) {
    sub <- H[order_out[1:k], idx, drop = FALSE]
    key <- apply(sub, 2, paste, collapse = "")
    n <- table(key)
    sum(n * (n - 1)) / (length(key) * (length(key) - 1))   # homozygosity of extended haplotypes
  })
}

Reading length as age

Recombination breaks haplotypes up at a roughly constant rate, so a long, unbroken haplotype is a young one – there has not been time to break it. As a rough guide, a haplotype of length L in morgans has an expected age of about 1/L generations. At roughly 1 centimorgan per megabase here, 50 kb is about 0.05 cM = 0.0005 M, giving an order-of-magnitude age of a couple of thousand generations – tens of thousands of years.

Treat that as an order of magnitude and nothing more. The calculation assumes a constant recombination rate, no selection, a single origin and a simple demography, and the real confidence interval is very wide. But it is the right order: old enough to be spread across Eurasia, young enough not to have been broken up.

1. Why a second dataset, and why this one

The 1000 Genomes genotypes are inferred in two ways. Reads a few hundred bases long are aligned to the GRCh38 reference, so anything that differs too much from the reference is hard to see at all; and the assignment of alleles to the two chromosomes – the phase – comes from a statistical model, not from observation.

The Human Pangenome Reference Consortium takes a different approach: for each person, long reads are assembled into two complete chromosome sequences, one per parent, and the assemblies are combined into a graph. Nothing is imputed. Where the two datasets agree, the finding is solid; where they disagree, the disagreement is informative.

A pangenome in one paragraph

A single reference genome is one person's sequence, and every variant is described as a difference from it. That works until the thing you care about is absent from the reference, or sits in a region where the reference is unusual. A pangenome graph holds many genomes at once: shared sequence is a single path, and where genomes differ the graph branches. Reads map to the graph rather than to one sequence, so a variant that is common in the species but missing from the reference is no longer invisible.

The answer, and a trap worth seeing

The block is not archaic. At every one of the fourteen positions where the comparison is valid, the Neanderthal looks like a non-carrier. A haplotype inherited from Neanderthals would not do that.

Now the trap. Two of the 20 variants are deletions, written in VCF style as ATAGGGGTC>A and TC>T – so the "haplotype allele" is the string A or T. The archaic file reports a single base per position, and at those two positions it reports A and T. A naive string comparison says "match" and reports that the Neanderthal carries the haplotype allele. It does not: the match is between a VCF spelling convention and a single base, and means nothing. Two apparent matches out of sixteen called positions is over 10% – enough to turn a clean negative into an ambiguous one and to let someone write a sentence about partial archaic ancestry.

The fix is to compare only single-base substitutions and say so. There is a second, quieter version of the same bug in the same comparison: the usual way to check that your coordinate conversion worked is to confirm that the archaic reference base matches the human reference allele – but if you compare the archaic base against only the first character of a multi-base allele, an indel passes that check automatically and tells you nothing. Compare the whole string, and count the indels separately.

The general lesson is worth more than either result: when two datasets describe the same thing in different formats, the join is where the errors live. Check a few by hand, always.

One archaic genome tested at fourteen positions is good evidence, not proof. 2. The textbook method, and why it fails here

The standard tool is approximate conditional analysis: given summary statistics and an LD matrix, estimate what each variant's z would be if you held the lead variant fixed. The formula, from Yang and colleagues (2012), is

z(i|C) = ( zi − RiCRCC−1zC ) ⁄ √( 1 − RiCRCC−1RCi )

Implement it with one conditioning variant, then two, then five, and watch what happens:

cond <- function(zi, zC, riC, RCC) {
  w <- solve(RCC, zC)
  num <- zi - sum(riC * w)
  den <- sqrt(max(1e-12, 1 - sum(riC * solve(RCC, riC))))
  num / den
}
# build the LD matrix in Europeans and condition on the top 1, 2, then 5 variants
Z <- t(scale(t(H[, eur])))
top <- order(-abs(d$z[match(pos, d$pos38)]), na.last = NA)[1:5]
target <- match(38666394, pos)                    # rs62242851, the block's strongest
for (k in 1:5) {
  C <- top[1:k]
  RCC <- (Z[C, , drop = FALSE] %*% t(Z[C, , drop = FALSE])) / sum(eur)
  riC <- as.vector((Z[target, ] %*% t(Z[C, , drop = FALSE])) / sum(eur))
  zc <- cond(d$z[match(pos[target], d$pos38)], d$z[match(pos[C], d$pos38)], riC, RCC)
  cat(sprintf("conditioning on %d variants: z = %7.2f  (P = %s)\n",
              k, zc, format.pval(2 * pnorm(-abs(zc)), digits = 2)))
}
Check

The answer does not settle down. As you add conditioning variants the conditional P-value swings over tens of orders of magnitude – in our run, from 10−6 to 10−14 to 10−42. Adding information makes the answer bigger, which is not how a well-behaved estimator behaves.

Why it breaks – and this is a real result, not a bug

The formula assumes the LD matrix R is known. It is not: it is estimated from 503 Europeans, and each correlation carries a standard error of roughly (1 − r²)/√n ≈ 0.03. The correction subtracted from your z is RiCRCC−1zC, and with |zC| ≈ 51 an error of 0.03 in r becomes an error of about 1.5 in the correction – comparable to the entire signal you are testing. Inverting RCC for correlated conditioning variants amplifies it further.

This is a known limitation of conditional analysis with an external LD reference, and it is at its worst exactly here: a gigantic lead signal, a small reference panel, and a weakly correlated variant of interest. The right response is not to pick whichever run gives the nicest answer. It is to use a method whose uncertainty you can see.

What to do when a standard method misbehaves

Report it. An analysis that says "the standard approach was unstable here, for this reason, so we did something more conservative" is far stronger than one that quietly reports the run that worked. If you ever find yourself choosing between several versions of an analysis on the basis of the answer, stop and write down what is happening – that moment is the single most common origin of results that do not replicate.

3. A diagnostic that shows its working

Turn the question round. Instead of asking "what is left after removing the lead?", ask "what would we expect to see if the lead were the only cause?" and compare. If rs6801957 is the sole causal variant, the expected signal at variant i is simply

zexpected = r × zlead

and because r is estimated, that expectation has a standard error of |zlead| × (1 − r²)/√n. Both go into the comparison:

tagging <- function(z_obs, z_lead, r, n) {
  z_exp <- r * z_lead
  se_exp <- abs(z_lead) * (1 - r^2) / sqrt(n)
  resid <- z_obs - z_exp
  se_resid <- sqrt(1 + se_exp^2)          # 1 for the observed z, se_exp for the expectation
  t <- resid / se_resid
  c(z_exp = z_exp, se_exp = se_exp, resid = resid, t = t, p = 2 * pnorm(-abs(t)))
}
round(tagging(z_obs = 7.78, z_lead = -51.5, r = -0.057, n = 1006), 4)
Check

For rs62242851 and the PR interval: expected z = +2.92 ± 1.62, observed +7.78, so a residual of +4.86 against a standard error of 1.90 – t = +2.55, P = 0.011. Notice how large the ±1.62 is. That is the honest uncertainty that conditional analysis had hidden.

Why this is the conservative choice

This diagnostic is deliberately weaker than conditional analysis. It conditions on one variant rather than several, so any signal carried by a second independent variant at the locus is left in the "expected" column and counted against you. It inflates the standard error by the full uncertainty of r. If a result survives this, it is not an artefact of the LD reference – and if it does not survive, you have not proved it absent, only that you cannot distinguish it from tagging.

5. Finding out whether the test works

A P-value is a statement about how often you would see something this extreme if the null hypothesis were true. So before believing P = 1.4 × 10−4, ask what this test's null hypothesis actually is. It is: the strongest variant in the window is the only causal one.

You already know that is false here. There are three independent signals, and one of them – rs4131768, r = 0.29 with your block – is not being conditioned on. So the real question is: how does this test behave when its null is wrong in that particular way?

That is a question about a sampling distribution, and you do not have to reason about it. You can simulate it and count.

Note what just happened to your own standards

This is the same single-lead test you have just shown to over-reject 86% of the time. So why is it acceptable here?

Because of which way its bias runs. The test is anti-conservative: it over-states independence. Here it found no independent signal – so a test biased towards finding one, finding none, is strong evidence that there is none. In section 4 it found a signal, which is exactly what the bias produces, so there the result is worthless.

The same test, the same data, opposite evidential value, depending only on the direction of the answer. Get into the habit of asking which way a method's bias points before deciding what its output is worth. It is not double standards – it is the only consistent standard there is.

What this stage was really teaching

Four things, in order of how often they go wrong in published work:

  1. A P-value is only as good as its null hypothesis, and the null is the part nobody checks.

2. The structure

Each section's actual job
  • Title. The finding, not the activity. "A 50 kb haplotype carries the SCN5A promoter variants…" not "An investigation of…".
  • Abstract. Four or five sentences: what was known, what you did, what you found with the key number, what it means. Every number in it must appear in the results.
  • Introduction. Three paragraphs is usually enough. What the locus is; what the 2012 paper reported; the specific gap – nobody asked whether those two variants are part of something larger. End with the question, not with a summary of your answer.
  • Methods. Enough for a stranger to repeat it. Data sources with accessions and versions; the genome build and how you converted; every threshold with its value; the software with version numbers. Write this section first – it is the easiest, and it tells you what you actually did.
  • Results. In the order the findings were established, each with its number and uncertainty, no interpretation. Hardest discipline in the paper: say what you found, not what it means.
  • Discussion. What it means, what it does not mean, how it fits the published literature, what you could not resolve, what should be done next. Limitations belong here, in the open, not in a defensive final paragraph.
  • Data and code availability. Where every input came from and where your scripts are.

4. Writing a limitation properly

Most students write limitations as apologies. They are not. A limitation is a precise statement of what your evidence cannot support, and it is the part of a paper that experienced readers trust most.

Weak and strong, on the same point

Weak: "A limitation of our study is that our LD reference panel was relatively small, so some of our conclusions may be less reliable."

Strong: "Correlations with the lead variant were estimated in 503 unrelated Europeans, giving a standard error of about 0.03. With zlead = −51.5 for the PR interval, this propagates to an uncertainty of ±1.6 on the expected z – comparable to the signal being tested. We therefore report the PR interval association as indistinguishable from tagging rather than as absent, and note that a reference panel of order 104 would be needed to resolve it."

The second is longer, and says something. It names the quantity, gives its size, traces its consequence, states what was done about it, and says what would settle it. Write all of your limitations like that.

Genotypes and association: PLINK 2

PLINK works with the genotypes of many people at once – thousands of people, millions of variants. It checks their quality, counts allele frequencies, measures linkage disequilibrium and relatedness, and tests each variant for association with a trait: a genome-wide association study, or GWAS. Every PLINK command has the same shape: the data (--vcf, --pfile, --bfile), filters, what to compute, and --out, the name of the files it writes.

The examples work in a folder of your own:

The data, and PLINK's own files

/data/examples/plink2/1kg-phase3-chr21.vcf.gz holds the genotypes of the 2,504 people of the 1000 Genomes Project (Phase 3) at the first 600 variants of chromosome 21 – real public data, and the test file of PLINK 2 itself. A VCF file has a column for each person:

zcat /data/examples/plink2/1kg-phase3-chr21.vcf.gz | grep -v '^##' | head -3 | cut -f 1-5,10-12
plink2 --vcf /data/examples/plink2/1kg-phase3-chr21.vcf.gz --make-pgen --out chr21
ls chr21.*

A genotype such as 0|1 gives the alleles of the two copies of the chromosome – 0 the reference allele (REF), 1 the alternative (ALT); | means phased: which copy carries which is known. --make-pgen writes PLINK 2's own files: chr21.pgen, the genotypes, packed tightly; chr21.pvar, the variants (the first columns of the VCF); chr21.psam, the people; and chr21.log, a record of what was done. --pfile chr21 reads the three back – much quicker than the VCF for every new command. PLINK 1's files, .bed, .bim and .fam, are still common: --make-bed writes them and --bfile reads them.

The log, and what PLINK prints, begin with the options in effect: besides yours, --threads 1 and --memory 640 require, which the terminal adds (see the end of this page).

Quality control: frequencies, missing genotypes, Hardy–Weinberg

plink2 --pfile chr21 --freq --missing --hardy --out qc
head -4 qc.afreq
awk 'NR > 1 && ($5 < 0.01 || $5 > 0.99)' qc.afreq | wc -l
tail -n +2 qc.hardy | sort -g -k10,10 | head -4

qc.afreq has the frequency of each ALT allele and the number of alleles counted – 5,008, two for each person. Most variants are rare: for 566 of the 600 the less common allele has a frequency below 1%, and hundreds were seen only once among the 5,008 chromosomes (a frequency of 0.0002). qc.smiss and qc.vmiss count the missing genotypes of each person and of each variant: none here, as the 1000 Genomes release gives every genotype – in data of your own, a person or a variant with many missing genotypes is a warning.

qc.hardy holds the Hardy–Weinberg exact test: are there as many heterozygotes as the allele frequency predicts? O(HET_A1) is the share of heterozygotes observed, E(HET_A1) the share expected, P the p-value. A few variants are very far from it, always with too many heterozygotes – 0.88 where 0.50 is expected. These variants lie on the short arm of chromosome 21, near its centromere, among repeated sequences – most likely, reads from two copies of a repeat were aligned to one place, and the difference between the copies looks like a heterozygous genotype in almost everyone. Such errors are what the Hardy–Weinberg filter is for.

Filters

plink2 --pfile chr21 --maf 0.01 --hwe 1e-6 --make-pgen --out chr21_qc

Filters act before anything else. --maf 0.01 keeps the variants whose minor allele frequency is 1% or more; --hwe 1e-6 removes those with a Hardy–Weinberg p-value below 10−6; --geno 0.02 would remove variants with more than 2% of their genotypes missing, and --mind 0.02 such people. The log says what each did: 25 variants removed by --hwe (all with too many heterozygotes), 566 by --maf, 9 remaining. --keep FILE and --remove FILE choose people by their IDs, --extract FILE and --exclude FILE variants, --chr with --from-bp and --to-bp a region.

Linkage disequilibrium

plink2 --pfile chr21_qc --indep-pairwise 50 5 0.2 --out ld
cat ld.prune.in
plink2 --pfile chr21_qc --r2-unphased --ld-window-r2 0.2 --out ld
cat ld.vcor

Variants close to each other are inherited together, so their genotypes are correlated: linkage disequilibrium, measured as r2. --indep-pairwise 50 5 0.2 moves along the chromosome in windows of 50 variants, 5 at a time, and of every pair with r2 above 0.2 keeps one: ld.prune.in lists the variants kept (6 of the 9), ld.prune.out the others. Analyses that expect independent variants – principal components, relatedness – use the pruned set (--extract ld.prune.in). --r2-unphased lists the pairs: rs559962037 and rs556687069, 229 bases apart, have r2 = 0.96 – they are nearly always inherited together.

Association: does a variant go with a trait?

The 1000 Genomes data has no trait to study, so these examples use synthetic data made for PLINK's own tests: 603 invented people, 30 variants (v1 … v30) and three phenotypes – BINARY (case 2 or control 1) and QUANT (a number), both drawn at random, and FIRTH, which makes every carrier of v1's ALT allele a case – with two covariates, C1 and C2 (in a real study: age, sex, principal components).

D=/data/examples/plink2
head -3 $D/synthetic_phenotypes.tsv
plink2 --vcf $D/synthetic_glm.vcf --pheno $D/synthetic_phenotypes.tsv --covar $D/synthetic_covariates.tsv --glm hide-covar --out assoc
ls assoc.*
cut -f 3,9,11-16 assoc.QUANT.glm.linear | head -5

--glm tests every variant with every phenotype: linear regression for a quantitative trait (assoc.QUANT.glm.linear), logistic regression for cases and controls (.glm.logistic.hybrid), with the covariates in each model (hide-covar: their own lines are left out). A line gives the variant, the allele tested (A1) and its frequency, the number of people, the effect – BETA, the change of the trait for each copy of A1, or OR, the odds ratio – its standard error, the test statistic and the p-value. QUANT and BINARY are random, so their p-values are spread between 0 and 1: of 30 tests, one or two fall below 0.05 by chance (here one for QUANT, none for BINARY). That is why a study is judged against the number of its tests – 0.05/30 here, and 5×10−8 for the million tests of a genome-wide study.

sort -g -k16,16 assoc.FIRTH.glm.logistic.hybrid | cut -f 3,10,13,16,17 | head -4

For FIRTH, v1 stands out. Every carrier of its ALT allele is a case – complete separation, for which ordinary logistic regression finds no odds ratio: it grows without end. PLINK then uses Firth's regression (FIRTH? Y), and still marks the result UNFINISHED: the odds ratio is in effect infinite. ERRCODE says when a result is not to be trusted as it stands. No other variant passes 0.05/30 = 0.0017. The p-values as a picture, in Python (a small "Manhattan plot"):

os.chdir(os.path.expanduser("~/gwas"))

                   ("FIRTH", "assoc.FIRTH.glm.logistic.hybrid")]:

Scores

head -3 /data/examples/plink2/synthetic_scores.tsv
plink2 --pfile chr21 --score /data/examples/plink2/synthetic_scores.tsv 1 2 3 header-read cols=+scoresums --out score
head -4 score.sscore

A score adds up, for each person, the copies of chosen alleles, each multiplied by a weight. --score FILE 1 2 3 takes the variant, the allele and the weight from columns 1, 2 and 3 of the file (header-read: its first line is a header). score.sscore has, for each person, the alleles counted (ALLELE_CT, two for each of the 20 variants), how many of them were the named allele, and the score – the average over the alleles (WEIGHT_AVG) and, with cols=+scoresums, the sum (WEIGHT_SUM). A polygenic risk score is made this way, with the effect sizes of a published GWAS as the weights. The weights of synthetic_scores.tsv are made up: these scores mean nothing.

More, and what is not here

  • --export vcf (vcf bgz: compressed) writes a VCF file again, --make-bed PLINK 1's files, and --export A a table of 0, 1 or 2 copies for each person and variant, to read in Python or R.
  • --make-king-table estimates the kinship of each pair of people (KING), and --king-cutoff removes one of each related pair. It needs thousands of common variants: on 600 variants, most of them rare, its numbers are noise. /data/examples/plink2/keep48.samples.txt lists 48 of the people, for --keep.

Intervals and annotation

BED and GFF files describe regions: genes, exons, peaks. bedtools compares and combines them; gffread converts annotations and writes the sequences of transcripts and proteins.

The files

grep -v "^#" /data/examples/annotation/cyp2c_slices.gff3 | cut -f 1-5,9 | head -4

Text tools

Most bioinformatics files are text. The GNU tools – grep, sed, awk, sort, cut, uniq, wc – answer many questions in one line, without a program of their own.

Look and count

Columns: cut, sort, uniq

# essential genes (column 3 of the yeast table: 1 = essential)
cut -d, -f3 /data/examples/tables/yeast_genes.csv | tail -n +2 | sort | uniq -c
# the variant positions of a VCF, as a list
bcftools view -H /data/examples/variants/NA12878.calls.vcf.gz | cut -f 1,2 | head -5

Careful with cut -d, on CSV files: a field with a comma inside quotes is cut there too. In the yeast table the location ("Golgi, vesicles & vacuole") and the description have such commas, so every column after the location – the chromosome is column 16 – would be counted wrong. gawk can read the quoted fields (FPAT describes what a field looks like); pandas and R read CSV files properly too:

awk -v FPAT='[^,]*|"[^"]*"' 'NR > 1 { print $16 }' /data/examples/tables/yeast_genes.csv | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head -5

grep -v "^#" /data/examples/annotation/cyp2c_slices.gff3 | cut -f 3 | sort | uniq -c

Scripts and pipelines

Commands that work once belong in a script – so that they run again tomorrow, on new data, and show exactly what was done.

The examples work in a folder of your own:

A bash script

Write the commands into a file – in the Files tab, or with a here-document as below – and run it with bash:

cat > count_reads.sh << 'EOF'
#!/usr/bin/env bash
set -euo pipefail          # stop at the first error
for f in "$@"; do
  n=$(( $(wc -l < "$f") / 4 ))
  echo -e "$(basename "$f")\t$n"
done
EOF
bash count_reads.sh /data/examples/reads/*.fastq

"$@" are the file names given to the script; set -euo pipefail makes it stop at the first command that fails, instead of carrying on with half a result. After chmod +x count_reads.sh it also starts as ./count_reads.sh.

Snakemake

Snakemake runs a pipeline from rules: each rule says which files it makes from which, and Snakemake runs only what is missing or out of date. The terminal has a browser edition of Snakemake 9 – snakemake -n shows the plan, snakemake --cores 1 runs it, snakemake --dag | dot -Tsvg -o dag.svg draws it:

cat > Snakefile << 'EOF'
rule all:
    input: "results/flagstat.txt"

rule map:
    input:
        ref="/data/examples/reads/reference.fa",

Asking from code

Each card uses a REST API: a web address goes in, data comes out – JSON for most, FASTA or text for sequences. The same works in a notebook:

import requests

r = requests.get("https://rest.ensembl.org/lookup/symbol/homo_sapiens/BHLHE41",
                 headers={"Content-Type": "application/json"})
r.raise_for_status()          # stop here if the service said no

gene["id"], gene["seq_region_name"], gene["start"], gene["end"]

In the browser, R can only make GET requests (a URL); for services that need a POST request – BLAST, VEP with positions, the PDB search – use Python. More in the recipe Databases from code.

Good practice

  • Databases change. New releases add, correct and remove entries; a query can give another answer next month. Note the date – the notebook of your queries does – and, for positions on a genome, the assembly: GRCh38 (hg38) and GRCh37 (hg19) number the same base differently.
  • Annotations are predictions. VEP calls rs4244285 synonymous – yet it is CYP2C19*2, a loss-of-function allele that changes the splicing of the gene (see Genes, genomes and variants). Look at the clinical significance and the literature before you conclude.
  • The services are shared by scientists everywhere. Ask for what you need, not thousands of queries in a quick loop: Ensembl allows about 15 requests a second, NCBI 3. A request that gets no answer at all is sent again (twice) by the tab; for code, see the recipe.
  • When a query fails, the message says why: Not found (404) – check the identifier; did not accept the query (400) – check what you typed; slow down (429) – wait a minute; not answering (5xx) or no answer that the page could read – the service is busy or down: try again later.

UniProt

UniProtKB, the UniProt Knowledgebase, is the reference collection of protein sequences and of what is known about each protein. The UniProt card shows its entries laid out like the pages of uniprot.org, from live data.

Reviewed and unreviewed

UniProtKB has two parts. Swiss-Prot (★ Reviewed) entries are read and written by expert curators, from the literature; there are about 575,000 of them. TrEMBL (Unreviewed) entries – about 150 million – are annotated automatically, mostly from genome sequences (numbers of release 2026_03). When a reviewed entry exists, use it.

Each entry has an accession (P04637), which never changes and is the one to cite, and an entry name (P53_HUMAN). Its annotation score (1 to 5) says how much is known about it, not how correct it is.

Protein Data Bank

The Protein Data Bank (PDB) holds the 3D structures of proteins, DNA, RNA and their complexes that have been determined by experiment – over 260,000 of them. Each has a four-character PDB ID, such as 1MBN. The card searches the RCSB PDB, shows an entry, and loads it into the 3D viewer.

Search

Type a PDB ID, a protein name, a UniProt accession or any words. Auto decides which it is; the menu next to it can say so instead (Protein name, UniProt accession, Full text). Narrow the results with Human only, Contains DNA and the method (X-ray, cryo-EM, NMR); sort by Best match, Newest first or Best resolution. The results come ten to a page, each with Load in 3D and Details.

An entry

  • Method and resolution. X-ray crystallography and cryo-EM give a resolution in ångström (Å): the smaller the number, the finer the detail. Roughly: at 1.2–1.5 Å and better single atoms can be told apart; around 2 Å the side chains are clear; beyond about 3.5 Å the path of the chain shows, and the side chains are largely modelled. NMR structures have no resolution; they are a set of models.
  • The paper that describes the structure.
  • Macromolecules: each kind of molecule, its chains (A, B …), type, length, organism, and the UniProt entry of the protein – click it to open it in the UniProt card.
  • Small molecules: ligands, ions and sugars, each with its three-letter code (HEM is haem, HOH water) and a drawing.
  • Biological assemblies: the file holds the asymmetric unit – the chains the experiment saw together – which is not always the molecule as it exists in the cell. The assembly says which it is (a monomer, a dimer …).
  • Files: Look inside the .pdb file shows its records with the columns coloured; Save .pdb to my files or Save .cif keeps the file in ~/structures; Send to notebook adds Python or R code that fetches the entry and its file.

The file

A structure is a text file with one line per atom. In the old .pdb format the columns are fixed:

ATOM      1  N   VAL A   1      -2.900  17.600  15.500  1.00  0.00           N
ATOM      2  CA  VAL A   1      -3.600  16.400  15.300  1.00  0.00           C

After ATOM and the atom's number come the atom name (CA is the alpha carbon), the residue (VAL), the chain (A), the residue number, the x, y and z coordinates in ångström, the occupancy and the B-factor (0 in this old file), and the element.

ATOM lines are the protein or nucleic acid, HETATM lines the ligands and water. The B-factor says how much an atom moves or how uncertain its position is (in AlphaFold models this column holds the pLDDT confidence instead). Very large structures exist only in the newer .cif (mmCIF) format. Both are read by the 3D viewer and by Biopython (Bio.PDB) in a Python notebook.

grep '^HETATM' /data/examples/structures/1mbn.pdb | awk '{print $4}' | sort | uniq -c

AlphaFold DB and InterPro

Two cards for what a protein's sequence says about it: AlphaFold DB has a predicted 3D structure for almost every protein in UniProt; InterPro finds its families, domains and important sites.

AlphaFold DB

AlphaFold predicts a protein's structure from its sequence. AlphaFold DB (EMBL-EBI and Google DeepMind) holds the predictions for over 200 million UniProt proteins. Type a UniProt accession and Search:

  • The table names the model (AF-P69905-F1), the protein and its organism, and the mean pLDDT.
  • The picture is the predicted aligned error (PAE).
  • Show in the 3D viewer loads the model, coloured by confidence; Save the model (.pdb) to my files keeps it in ~/structures.

How far to trust a model

pLDDTColourMeaning
above 90dark bluevery high confidence: side chains placed well too
70 – 90light blueconfident: the backbone is right
50 – 70yellowlow confidence
below 50orangevery low: often a region with no fixed structure (disordered), not a real shape

pLDDT is a confidence for each residue, about its own surroundings. It says nothing about how the domains lie relative to each other: that is what the PAE plot shows. Its colour in column x (the scored residue, along the top) and row y (the aligned residue, down the side) is the error AlphaFold expects in the position of residue x when the model is lined up on residue y: dark is a small error. Dark squares along the diagonal are domains placed confidently; light areas between them mean their arrangement is uncertain.

In the 3D viewer the model's panel has the pLDDT profile (click it to go to a residue), the PAE plot (drag a box over it: the two stretches of residues are selected, with the mean expected error between them), Hide pLDDT < 50, and Superpose onto… an experimental structure. A model is a prediction: where an experimental structure exists, compare them.

Genes, genomes and variants

Three cards for genes, genomes and sequencing data: Ensembl, NCBI and the European Nucleotide Archive.

Ensembl

Ensembl (EMBL-EBI) annotates the genomes of vertebrates and model organisms: genes, transcripts, proteins, variants, and how genes of different species are related. The card asks its REST API, which serves the latest release – for human, on the GRCh38 assembly.

Gene
A species and a gene symbol (BHLHE41) or Ensembl gene ID (ENSG…). The answer gives the gene's ID, type (biotype), position and strand, and a table of its transcripts – which is the canonical one, how many exons, how long the protein is – with buttons that save each transcript's cDNA, CDS or protein sequence. Save the gene sequence (FASTA); for human genes, Show in the Genome browser (GRCh38).
Sequence
The sequence of an Ensembl gene, transcript or protein ID. genomic: the whole region, introns included (with extra bases on each side if you ask); cDNA: the exons of a transcript, untranslated regions included; CDS: from the start codon to the stop codon; protein.
Variant effect (VEP)
The Variant Effect Predictor: an rs ID (rs4244285), an HGVS description (ENST00000646891.2:c.1799T>A), or a chromosome, position, reference and alternative base on GRCh38 (10 94781859 G A). The answer gives the most severe consequence; the known variants at that position, with their clinical significance; allele frequencies (1000 Genomes, gnomAD); and for each transcript the consequence, its impact (HIGH, MODERATE, LOW, MODIFIER), the amino-acid change and the SIFT and PolyPhen predictions. A consequence is worked out from the position and the transcripts alone: rs4244285 comes out as synonymous (Pro227Pro), yet it is CYP2C19*2, the commonest loss-of-function allele of the gene – the base change makes a new splice site, and the mRNA is spliced wrongly. Read the clinical significance and the papers too.
Orthologues
The genes in another species – or in all of them – that descend from one gene of their last common ancestor: orthologues are separated by a speciation event (paralogues by a duplication within a genome).
Genes in a region
Every gene between two positions of a chromosome (at most 5 Mb).
GRCh38 and GRCh37

The same base has different positions on the two human assemblies: rs121912617, the DNA change behind DEC2 P384R, is at chr12:26,122,364 on GRCh38 and chr12:26,275,297 on GRCh37 (hg19). gnomAD v4 and this project use GRCh38; many older papers use GRCh37. In code, Ensembl's GRCh37 server is https://grch37.rest.ensembl.org.

European Nucleotide Archive

ENA (EMBL-EBI) – with its partners NCBI's SRA and DDBJ – keeps the raw data of published sequencing experiments. Sequencing runs takes a study (PRJEB37886), sample or run (SRR10331604, the Brazilian porcupine of stage 10) accession, or a query in ENA's language (tax_eq(9606) AND library_strategy="AMPLICON"), and lists the runs: organism, instrument, library strategy and layout, number of reads, and the FASTQ files. Mind the size

A human genome or exome run is many gigabytes (the two FASTQ files of SRR098401 are 7.5 and 7.7 GB): far more than fits in a browser. Small runs – of viruses, bacteria, amplicons – are a few megabytes to a few hundred. The recipe Databases from code shows how to take just the first reads of a large file.

BLAST

BLAST finds the sequences in a database that are similar to yours – the first thing to do with an unknown sequence. The BLAST card sends the search to EMBL-EBI, where it runs for seconds to a few minutes, and shows the hits.

A search

  1. Program – which depends on what your sequence is and what you search:
    ProgramYour sequenceSearches
    blastpproteinproteins
    blastnDNADNA
    blastxDNA, translated in all six reading framesproteins

    Protein searches find relatives much further back in evolution than DNA searches: when the sequence codes for a protein, compare proteins (blastp, or blastx from DNA).

ColumnMeans

The IDE tab: RStudio and Spyder

Most of your work in the workbench will be done in notebooks, but some jobs are easier in an integrated development environment, or IDE. The IDE tab has two of them: RStudio for R and Spyder for Python. Both are imitations of the real programs, made for teaching, and they work in the same way as far as possible, so what you learn here carries over to RStudio and Spyder on your own computer.

A notebook or an IDE?

A notebook is the best place to explore your data. Each piece of code sits next to its results, and you can write notes in between, so the notebook becomes a record of what you did and what you found. An IDE is built for writing programs rather than records, and it is worth switching to one when:

  • your code has grown beyond a few cells, and you would rather keep it in a script that you can run again from the top whenever your data change;
  • you are writing a report in R Markdown or Quarto, or an interactive Shiny app (both in RStudio);
  • your code doesn’t do what you expected, and you want to go through it one line at a time and watch your variables change (Spyder’s debugger);
  • you want to see all of your variables at a glance, and look inside data frames and arrays as tables;
  • you will be using RStudio or Spyder on your own computer later, and want to get to know them first.

The two ways of working go well together. You can try something out in a notebook and then copy the code that works into a script, or write functions in a script and load them into a notebook with source("functions.R") in R or %run functions.py in Python.

Each IDE has its own R or Python

RStudio and Spyder each run their own copy of R or Python, separate from the one that the notebooks use, just as RStudio and Spyder start R and Python processes of their own on a computer. A variable that you create in a notebook therefore doesn’t exist in Spyder, and the other way round: if you need it in both places, run the code that creates it in both. What they do share are your files. A script in Spyder can read a table that a notebook wrote, and a notebook can open a plot that RStudio saved.

The first time you open an IDE, your browser has to download R or Python, which can take a minute or so (R alone is about 30 MB). After that it keeps a copy, so the IDE opens much more quickly on your next visit. An IDE that you have opened keeps running while you use the other tabs, and you can have both open at once: the buttons at the top of the IDE tab switch between them.

Opening your files in an IDE

  • In the Files tab, an R script or an R Markdown or Quarto document has an Open in RStudio button above it, and a Python script has Open in Spyder. You will find the same commands in the menu of a file (its ⋯ button, or a right-click).
  • In the Terminal, rstudio analysis.R and spyder script.py open files in the IDE, and rstudio or spyder on its own simply opens it.
  • Inside an IDE, use its own File ▸ Open (Ctrl+O) or its Files pane.

As in the real programs, a file that you edit in an IDE is saved only when you save it (Ctrl+S). RStudio shows the name of a file with unsaved changes in red, and Spyder adds an asterisk to it. If you try to leave or reload the page while a file has unsaved changes, your browser asks whether you really want to.

The AI assistant and the IDEs

When code fails in RStudio or Spyder, a ✦ Ask the AI assistant about this error button appears under the error. It sends the code and the error message to the assistant, together with the script you are working on, the end of the console, and the names and types of your variables (but not their values). If you go from the IDE tab to the assistant and ask a question of your own, the same information goes with it, unless you untick your code in the IDE under the message box. Code in the assistant’s answers can then go straight into the script that is open in RStudio or Spyder, with Put into RStudio or Put into Spyder. See The AI assistant for how to connect a model.

What is different from the real programs

Everything in the IDE tab runs inside your web browser, and that brings a few limits that RStudio and Spyder on a computer don’t have:

  • R and Python can’t start other programs, so system() in R and subprocess in Python don’t work. Instead, a line that starts with ! in Spyder’s console runs a command in the workbench’s terminal, and RStudio has a Terminal tab of its own next to the Console.
  • Spyder’s debugger and input(), and stopping code while it is running in either IDE, need the page to be cross-origin isolated. The workbench arranges this itself in most browsers (see when the debugger is not available).
  • Reports can be knitted to web pages (HTML), but not to PDF or Word, because the programs needed for those (pandoc and LaTeX) don’t run in a browser.
  • There are no projects, no version control (Git) and no plug-ins or add-ins.

RStudio is made by Posit, and Spyder by the Spyder project. The IDEs in this tab are not made or endorsed by either of them.

Scripts

A script is a text file of R commands, saved with the extension .R. To start one, choose File ▸ New File ▸ R Script, or click New script in RStudio under this example, which reads the workbench’s table of yeast genes and draws a plot:

genes <- read.csv("/data/examples/tables/yeast_genes.csv")
str(genes)

# how many genes in each part of the cell are essential (1) or not (0)?

boxplot(protein_length ~ essential, data = genes, log = "y",

When the debugger is not available

The debugger, input() and stopping code with Ctrl+C all need Python to pause and wait for you while it is in the middle of running something. A web page can only do that when it is cross-origin isolated. The workbench arranges this itself in most browsers, but not in every situation (in some private windows, for example). If the page is not isolated, the console says so when it starts. Everything else still works, but input() stops with an error instead of waiting for an answer, the debugger can’t be started, and the only way to stop code that runs for too long is Restart kernel. Reloading the page, or opening it in an ordinary window, usually helps; What is different in the browser explains more.

What is different from Spyder on a computer

  • The console’s Python runs inside your browser (Pyodide), and is separate from the Python of the notebooks.
  • Python can’t start threads or other processes, so threading, multiprocessing and subprocess don’t work; use !command for the programs of the terminal.
  • Figures are drawn in the Plots pane and the console only: there are no separate interactive windows, and desktop toolkits such as tkinter and Qt are not available.
  • There are no projects, no plug-ins and no version control.

The AI agent

Give the agent a whole task – map the example reads, call the variants and tell me how many there are. It carries it out in the Terminal, one command at a time, reads what each command prints before it decides the next, and reports. Every run is kept as a notebook.

Give it a task

  1. Connect a model first (see The AI assistant), then open the Agent tab.
  2. Type a task – or click one under Try a task – and Send.
  3. Watch: each step shows the command and what it printed, here and in the Terminal. Stop ends the task at any time.
  4. Read its report – then check it (below).

The agent works in a folder of its own, ~/ai-agent/run-1, run-2 …; programs put the files they make there. A further task goes on in the same folder, and the agent is told what was done before – until New starts a new folder.

What it may do

  • Run the commands of the terminal: the bioinformatics programs, the text tools, python and Rscript scripts, Snakemake. Not cd – every step starts in its folder, and it uses paths instead.
  • Read your files. What it changes outside its folder – a file it deletes or overwrites by mistake – is put back at the end of each step. What you change yourself while it works is yours, and stays.

Limits: at most 20 steps for a task; a command that runs for more than 150 seconds is stopped; the model sees the last 3,000 characters of what each command prints. While the agent works, the terminal is its own: wait, or press Stop.

From a protein to its structure

Sperm whale myoglobin, from its UniProt entry to its structure: the sequence and its properties, the haem pocket of the experimental structure 1MBN measured with Biopython, and the AlphaFold model compared with the experiment.

1. The UniProt entry

import requests

acc = "P02185"
entry = requests.get(f"https://rest.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/{acc}.json").json()
print(entry["uniProtkbId"], "–", entry["proteinDescription"]["recommendedName"]["fullName"]["value"])
print(entry["organism"]["scientificName"], "·", entry["sequence"]["length"], "amino acids")

# the binding sites UniProt lists: which residue binds what
for f in entry["features"]:
    if f["type"] == "Binding site":
        print(f["location"]["start"]["value"], f.get("ligand", {}).get("name", ""), f.get("description", ""))

His 65 binds the oxygen, His 94 the haem's iron. UniProt numbers the sequence from its first methionine; that methionine is removed in the mature protein, so the structure numbers every residue one lower (UniProt's His 65 is His 64 in 1MBN).

2. The sequence and its properties

from io import StringIO
from Bio import SeqIO
from Bio.SeqUtils.ProtParam import ProteinAnalysis

fasta = requests.get(f"https://rest.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/{acc}.fasta").text
record = SeqIO.read(StringIO(fasta), "fasta")
p = ProteinAnalysis(str(record.seq))
print(f"molecular weight {p.molecular_weight():.0f} Da, isoelectric point {p.isoelectric_point():.2f}, GRAVY {p.gravy():.3f}")
helix, turn, sheet = p.secondary_structure_fraction()
print(f"amino acids that favour helix: {helix:.0%}")

3. The haem pocket of 1MBN

The example file 1mbn.pdb is the first protein structure solved at high resolution (1960s). The haem's iron is held by His 93 below it – the proximal histidine – while His 64 above it lines the pocket where oxygen binds.

from Bio.PDB import PDBParser

s = PDBParser(QUIET=True).get_structure("1MBN", "/data/examples/structures/1mbn.pdb")
chain = s[0]["A"]
iron = next(a for a in s.get_atoms() if a.element == "FE")
for resno in (64, 93):
    res = chain[resno]
    print(f"{res.get_resname()} {resno}: NE2 is {iron - res['NE2']:.2f} Å from the iron")

# every residue with an atom within 5 Å of the haem
haem = [r for r in chain if r.get_resname() == "HEM"][0]
near = sorted({r.id[1] for r in chain for a in r for b in haem
               if r is not haem and r.id[0] == " " and a - b < 5})
print(len(near), "residues within 5 Å of the haem:", near)

4. The AlphaFold model

AlphaFold's file keeps its confidence, pLDDT, in the B-factor column.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

info = requests.get(f"https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/api/prediction/{acc}").json()[0]
with open(f"{info['entryId']}.pdb", "w") as fh:
    fh.write(requests.get(info["pdbUrl"]).text)
model = PDBParser(QUIET=True).get_structure("AF", f"{info['entryId']}.pdb")
plddt = [res["CA"].get_bfactor() for res in model[0]["A"]]

plt.figure(figsize=(6, 2.5))
plt.plot(range(1, len(plddt) + 1), plddt)
plt.axhline(70, ls="--", c="grey")
plt.xlabel("residue (UniProt numbering)")
plt.ylabel("pLDDT")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

5. Model and experiment, superposed

The Cα atoms of the model (residue n + 1) and of 1MBN (residue n) are laid on each other as closely as they go; the RMSD (root-mean-square deviation: the square root of the mean of the squared distances) then says how far apart they are.

from Bio.PDB import Superimposer

exp_ca, model_ca = [], []
af_chain = model[0]["A"]
for res in chain:
    n = res.id[1]
    if res.id[0] == " " and "CA" in res and (n + 1) in af_chain:
        exp_ca.append(res["CA"])
        model_ca.append(af_chain[n + 1]["CA"])
sup = Superimposer()
sup.set_atoms(exp_ca, model_ca)
print(f"RMSD over {len(exp_ca)} Cα atoms: {sup.rms:.2f} Å")

An RMSD around 1 Å means the prediction matches the experiment closely. To see it, run in the 3D viewer: load 1MBN; load af P02185; superpose AF-P02185-F1:A onto 1MBN:A (Viewer commands). The viewer reports a slightly smaller RMSD over a few residues fewer: it pairs the residues by aligning the sequences, leaves out pairs that end up more than 2 Å apart (loose ends) and fits again, as structure programs often do – the code above keeps every pair.

Databases from code

The Databases tab asks one question at a time. Code asks a hundred – and keeps the record. This recipe shows the patterns: a request and its answer as a table, many at once, a polite loop, what to do when a service is busy, NCBI with Biopython, and the first reads of a file too big to download.

1. A request that tries again

Web services are shared and sometimes busy: now and then they answer 429 (too many requests) or 5xx (a problem of the server), or nothing at all. A small function waits and asks again – and stops with a clear error for anything else (a wrong identifier gives 400 or 404).

import time
import requests
import pandas as pd

def ask(method, url, tries=4, **kwargs):
    """Make a request; when the service is busy or does not answer, wait and try again."""
    for attempt in range(1, tries + 1):
        try:
            r = requests.request(method, url, **kwargs)
            if r.status_code not in (429, 500, 502, 503, 504):
                r.raise_for_status()          # 400, 404 …: a mistake in the request – stop
                return r
            problem = f"HTTP {r.status_code}"
        except requests.exceptions.ConnectionError:
            problem = "no answer"
        if attempt == tries:
            raise RuntimeError(f"{problem} from {url} after {tries} tries – try again later")
        print(f"{problem} – trying again in {2 ** attempt} s")
        time.sleep(2 ** attempt)

def get_json(url, params=None):
    return ask("GET", url, params=params, headers={"Accept": "application/json"}).json()

gene = get_json("https://rest.ensembl.org/lookup/symbol/homo_sapiens/CYP2C19")
gene["id"], gene["seq_region_name"], gene["start"], gene["end"]

6. The first reads of a big file

A sequencing run is usually far too big for the browser – the reads of SRR098401 are 7.5 GB per file. A Range request downloads only the start of a compressed file, and zlib unpacks as much as arrived:

import zlib

url = "https://ftp.sra.ebi.ac.uk/vol1/fastq/SRR098/SRR098401/SRR098401_1.fastq.gz"
r = ask("GET", url, headers={"Range": "bytes=0-4999999"})      # the first 5 MB
text = zlib.decompressobj(16 + zlib.MAX_WBITS).decompress(r.content).decode()
lines = text.split("\n")
keep = (len(lines) - 1) // 4 * 4                                # whole reads only
with open("SRR098401_start_1.fastq", "w") as fh:
    fh.write("\n".join(lines[:keep]) + "\n")
print(keep // 4, "reads")
!head -n 8 SRR098401_start_1.fastq

The first reads of a run come from the edge of the flow cell, and are often poor (all N): for a fair sample, use reads from the middle of the file, or trim and filter them first.

What is different in the browser

Everything in the workbench runs on your own computer, inside this web page: the programs are the real ones, compiled to WebAssembly, and Python and R are the real languages. That brings some limits a server would not have.

Speed and memory

  • Each program runs on one processor core; options for threads (-t, -p, -@, -j, --threads, --cpu) are ignored, with a note. One program is different: PLINK 2 reads its files on threads of its own and computes on one or two (--threads 2 at most) – see programs on several threads. Programs run more slowly than on a server, and the speed depends on your computer: keep the data small.
  • A program, Python and R each have a few gigabytes of memory at most (WebAssembly is 32-bit: 4 GB is the ceiling, and browsers often allow less). Mapping reads to a whole human genome does not fit; regions, a few genes, bacterial and viral genomes do.
  • Python has no threads, no multiprocessing and no subprocess; input() does not work in a notebook, though it does in Spyder (in the IDE tab). R cannot run system(): use a %%bash cell.

Programs on several threads

PLINK 2 starts threads that share the program's memory. A browser allows that only in a page that is cross-origin isolated: the page and its workers come with two headers, Cross-Origin-Opener-Policy and Cross-Origin-Embedder-Policy, which also keep pictures of other sites out unless those sites allow them. Static hosting (GitHub Pages) cannot send the headers, so the workbench adds them itself with a small service worker (isolation-sw.js; it stores nothing): the first time you open the workbench, the page loads once more, at once. serve.py sends the headers itself.

  • If PLINK 2 says that the page is not isolated, reload the page. It cannot be isolated when it was not opened over https (or from localhost), or when the browser does not allow service workers there – some private windows do not.
  • The other programs, Python and R do not need isolation, and work either way. In the IDE tab, Spyder’s debugger and input(), and stopping code while it runs, need it too (see Spyder).
  • To turn isolation off – if a picture of another site does not show, say – open the workbench with ?isolation=off at the end of its address; open it without that to turn it on again.

Programs that are partly here

  • PLINK 2 is built without the LAPACK library, so --pca and --pheno-svd are not available (PLINK says so and stops). It works in 640 MiB of memory, the least it accepts: the terminal adds --threads 1 --memory 640 require unless you give them, and a larger --memory becomes 1024 (more threads than 2 become 2), with a note. --script and --rerun, which read options from a file, are not available: plink2 $(cat FILE) does the same. Its genotypes must fit in that memory – thousands of people and variants, not a whole biobank. It needs a cross-origin isolated page.

Files

  • Your files are kept in this browser on this computer, not on a server – see where your files are kept.
  • Browsers give a site a share of the free disk space – usually several gigabytes; My files shows how much is used. Files of hundreds of megabytes work, but slowly. Python and R are not given files over 300 MB, and a file over 1 GB is not kept in the browser at all: it is there while the page is open – download it if you need it.
  • Large intermediate files fill the space fast: pipe programs together (minimap2 … | samtools sort -o mapped.bam -) instead of writing a SAM file, and delete what you no longer need.

Installing

  • No sudo, apt or background jobs (&). Programs cannot be added to the terminal, and there is no need to: every program is installed already, so there is no conda here either. (To set up the same programs on another computer, see Keep and share your work.)
  • Python packages install with %pip install (pure-Python packages, and those Pyodide has built); R packages with install.packages() (those built for webR). Both last until the page is reloaded – except in RStudio, which remembers the R packages you installed there and fetches them again when library() needs them.
  • Some packages have no build for the browser yet – mostly those that need system libraries; the error says so.

The internet

  • Python is downloaded when a Python notebook is open, R when an R notebook is open – and the page opens the last notebook you had open when it starts (a Python one on the first visit) –, and again for Spyder or RStudio when you first open one of them in the IDE tab; packages when they are imported or installed: tens of megabytes, of which the browser keeps a copy for later visits.
  • A web page can only ask web services that allow requests from web pages (CORS). The big bioinformatics services do – Ensembl, UniProt, NCBI, EBI, the PDB, AlphaFold DB and the others of the Databases tab; KEGG and most web pages do not. This holds for notebooks, curl and wget alike.
  • R in the browser can only make GET requests; use Python for POST requests.
  • The Databases tab, the AI assistant and the structures of the PDB and AlphaFold DB need the internet all the time; the project's own data come with the site.

The tabs

  • Snakemake is a browser edition of its commonly used core: rules, wildcards, expand, -n, --dag, --summary; no cluster execution.
  • The IDE tab: RStudio and Spyder are imitations, made for teaching, of the programs of the same names. Each has an R or a Python of its own, separate from the notebooks’. Reports are knitted to HTML only (pandoc and LaTeX, which PDF and Word need, do not run in a browser); Shiny apps need the page’s service worker; there are no projects, no Git and no plug-ins (The IDE tab).
  • The 3D viewer needs WebGL (3D graphics in the browser): if it does not start, turn on hardware acceleration in the browser's settings.
  • The workbench is made for a laptop or desktop screen; on a phone or tablet it works, cramped.

Use a current Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari.

When something goes wrong

The usual problems, and what to do about them. Error messages in the notebook and the terminal often carry a hint of their own – and Ask the AI assistant about this error explains them (AI assistant).

Starting

Python or R takes long to start
The first start of a visit downloads the language (tens of megabytes); later visits use the browser's copy and are quicker. The label at the top right of a notebook says what it is doing.
A tab says it could not start
Reload the page (your files are kept). For the 3D viewer: turn on hardware acceleration in the browser's settings. Use a current Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari.
The page is slow or stops answering
A long computation is running: Stop it (notebook), or press Ctrl+C in the terminal – once, and what follows the running program is skipped; twice, and the program itself is stopped. Close other heavy tabs of the browser.

Files

My files are gone
They are kept in one browser on one computer – is it the same browser, the same computer, the same web address, and not a private window? If the browser's data for the site was cleared, they cannot be recovered: download what you want to keep (Keep and share your work).
"No such file or directory"
The file is not where the code looked. A notebook works in its own folder (~/notebooks): use full paths such as /data/examples/… or /home/user/… (Python's open() does not understand ~). %pwd or getwd() shows the folder; the Files tab shows the names.
"Read-only file system" or "Permission denied"
/data/examples is read-only: its files cannot be changed, and nothing new can be written into it – not even what a program writes next to its input (samtools faidx writes its index next to the FASTA file). Copy what you need into your home folder and work there.
A PDF "cannot be read as a PDF"
The Files tab draws PDF files itself, so no PDF plug-in of the browser is needed. A file it cannot read is damaged, is not a PDF, or is not finished: a plot that R or Python is still drawing into is written only when it is closed – dev.off() after pdf("plot.pdf") in R, plt.savefig("plot.pdf") or the end of with PdfPages(…) in Python. Download the file to open it with another program.
The space is running out
My files (top right) shows how much is used. Delete large intermediate files – SAM files, unsorted BAM files, trimmed reads – or download and delete them.

Programs and code

"command not found"
The program is not in the workbench: help lists what is. Python packages: %pip install NAME; R packages: install.packages("NAME").
plink2: "… this page is not" cross-origin isolated
PLINK 2 works on several threads, which a browser allows only in a cross-origin isolated page. Reload the page: the workbench isolates it as it starts. It cannot when the page was not opened over https (or from localhost), or in a browser window that allows no service workers (some private windows) – see programs on several threads.
A variant caller says an index is missing, or that the chromosomes do not match
Index the BAM files (samtools index) and the reference (samtools faidx) – again after a program has written a new BAM file. The reference must be the one the reads were mapped to, with the sequence names of the BAM header (samtools view -H file.bam | grep @SQ).
plink2: "--pca requires plink2 to be built with LAPACK"
This build of PLINK 2 has no LAPACK, the library its principal components (--pca) and --pheno-svd need: they are not available here. The rest of PLINK works. See Genotypes and association.
plink2 --glm: "invoked without --covar … usually an analytical mistake"
PLINK asks for covariates in an association test – ancestry (principal components), sex, age – because a test without them is usually confounded. Give them with --covar FILE, or, when you are sure, add the modifier: --glm allow-no-covars.
plink2: "Failed to open NAME.psam" (or .pgen, .pvar, .bed …)
--pfile NAME reads three files, NAME.pgen, NAME.pvar and NAME.psam (--bfile NAME: .bed, .bim, .fam): give the name without its ending, and make the files first (plink2 --vcf FILE.vcf.gz --make-pgen --out NAME).
A program says it is out of memory, or stops without a message
The data is too big for the browser: take a part of it (samtools view of a region, seqtk sample) – see What is different in the browser.
Python or R forgot my variables
The language was restarted – by Restart, Stop, or a reload of the page. Run the notebook's cells again from the top (Run all).
The terminal does not let me type
The AI agent is working in it: wait, or press Stop in the AI assistant tab.

The internet

"Failed to fetch", "no answer", a network error
Either the internet connection is off, or the site does not allow requests from web pages (CORS – KEGG and most web pages do not). Download such files with your browser and upload them in the Files tab.
HTTP 429, "too many requests"
Wait a minute; in code, pause between requests (Databases from code).
HTTP 400 or 404
The service did not understand the query, or there is no such identifier: check the spelling and the version (NM_000769.4).
HTTP 500 or 503
The service is busy or down – try again later.

The other tabs

RStudio or Spyder takes a long time to open, or says it could not be opened
The first time, the IDE downloads R or Python, which can take a minute or so on a slow connection. If it fails, check your internet connection and press Try again, or reload the page; your files are kept.
My variable is missing in RStudio or Spyder (or in the notebook)
Each IDE has an R or a Python of its own, separate from the notebooks’, so a variable only exists where you created it. Run the code that creates it in the IDE as well (The IDE tab).
Spyder: input() or the debugger doesn’t work
Both need the page to be cross-origin isolated, and the console says so when it starts if it isn’t. Reload the page, or open it in an ordinary (not private) window (Spyder).
RStudio: "there is no package called …"
In RStudio, as on your own computer, a package has to be installed once before library() can load it: run install.packages("NAME"), or use Tools ▸ Install Packages… (RStudio).
The AI assistant says "Not connected", the key is not accepted, or it is busy
Check the service and key in AI settings and press Test. Free keys allow only a few requests per minute: wait a minute.

Reporting a problem

Say which browser and computer you use, what you did, and what happened – with the exact message (copy it, or take a screenshot). Leave out any personal data.

Your data and privacy

The workbench has no server of its own that keeps or sees your work: it runs in your browser. What leaves your computer goes to the public services you use, and only when you use them.

What is kept on your computer

  • Your files – notebooks, results, uploads, your logbook – in this browser's storage for the site (IndexedDB).
  • In the browser's local storage: your last 200 terminal commands; the boxes you ticked in the guide's checklists; settings – the open tab and notebook, the help page, the genome, the choices of the AI assistant (not its key), your name in the workbench if you put one in, and your e-mail address for BLAST if you ask to remember it.
  • The AI key: only in the tab's memory, or, if you tick it, until the tab is closed (session storage). Never in a file or in long-term storage.

On a shared computer, the next person who uses the same browser account can see what is stored: download your work, then My files → Start afresh. That deletes your files, the terminal's commands and the remembered e-mail address; your name in the workbench and the choices of the AI assistant and of the help pane stay, and the AI key is forgotten when you close the tab. The workbench itself sets no cookies and has no tracking or analytics.

Personal and patient data

The workbench is for learning, with public data. Never put personal or patient data into it – not into the Databases tab, not into an AI tool, not into your files here. Data you have been given for your studies or your work stays within the rules you were given with it.
